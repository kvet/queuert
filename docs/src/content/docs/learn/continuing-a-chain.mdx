A chain can run more than one job: a job hands the chain on to the next job type, and the chain completes with the output of its last job.

<Diagram label="Chain: provision-account continues with send-welcome-email, and the chain completes">
  <Flow>
    <Node status="completed">provision-account</Node>
    <Arrow label="continueWith" />
    <Node status="completed">send-welcome-email</Node>
    <Arrow label="output" />
    <Node status="completed">chain completed</Node>
  </Flow>
</Diagram>

A handler ends its job through `applyJobOutcome` in one of two ways: with an `output`, which completes the chain, or with `continueWith`, the next `typeName` and that job's `input`, which creates the next job in the same transaction as the one that finished. The chain keeps its identity across jobs, so `client.awaitChain` resolves once the last job completes, with that job's output.

Types spell this out in `defineJobTypes`. A job type lists the types it may continue with in `continueWith` instead of declaring an `output`, and a type reachable only through a chain drops `entry`, so `createChain` refuses it. The compiler then checks every `continueWith` against the declared type and its input.

A job can also choose its next job at runtime. List every type it may continue with as a union, `continueWith: { typeName: "page-on-call" | "queue-for-support" }`, and pass the one you picked to `continueWith`; TypeScript checks the input against the type you chose, and only that job is created.

This lesson's definitions live in `job-types.ts` and its processors in `index.ts`. Switch between them with the file buttons above the editor.
