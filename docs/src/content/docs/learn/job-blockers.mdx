A job can wait for other chains: it starts as `blocked` and becomes `pending` once every chain it is blocked by has completed.

<Diagram label="Completed capture-payment and reserve-stock chains unblock ship-order, which moves from blocked to pending">
  <Flow>
    <Rows spine="end" columns="auto minmax(2.5rem, auto)">
      <Flow>
        <Node status="completed">capture-payment</Node>
        <Arrow head="none" />
      </Flow>
      <Flow>
        <Node status="completed">reserve-stock</Node>
        <Arrow head="none" />
      </Flow>
    </Rows>
    <Node sub={<Transition from="blocked" to="pending" />}>ship-order</Node>
  </Flow>
</Diagram>

A job type declares what it waits for in `blockers`, a tuple of job type references: `blockers: [{ typeName: "capture-payment" }, { typeName: "reserve-stock" }]`. `client.createChain` then takes the chains to wait for in `blockers`, and TypeScript checks them against the tuple. Create the blockers and the blocked chain in one transaction, so either all of them exist or none do.

When the blocked job runs, `job.blockers` holds the blocker chains in the same order, each with its typed `output`, so a blocked job can read what its blockers produced, such as the payment ID. A blocker's chain can be as long as you like: the job waits for its last job to complete.

Below, a worker runs three jobs at once. `ship-order` has no blockers, so it ships the order straight away, before the payment is captured and the stock is reserved.

<TryIt lesson="job-blockers">in `job-types.ts`, give `ship-order` the `blockers` `capture-payment` and `reserve-stock`; then pass the payment and stock chains as `blockers` when `index.ts` creates the `ship-order` chain.</TryIt>
